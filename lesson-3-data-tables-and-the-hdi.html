Measuring wellbeing: two kinds of indicator

Quantitative

Measured in numbers.

  • Life expectancy: 83.3 years
  • Expected years of schooling
  • Income per person in US$

Qualitative

Described in words: people’s feelings, opinions and experiences.

  • “I feel safe in my neighbourhood.”
  • How happy people say they are
  • Satisfaction with local services

The Human Development Index (HDI)

DimensionIndicator usedWhat it tells us
HealthLife expectancy at birthHow long people can expect to live
EducationExpected years of schooling (children) and mean years of schooling (adults)How much education people get
Standard of livingIncome per person (gross national income, adjusted for the cost of living)How much people can afford

The HDI combines these into one number from 0 to 1. Groups: Very high 0.800 and above · High 0.700–0.799 · Medium 0.550–0.699 · Low below 0.550.

Choral response“The three dimensions of the HDI are health, education and …” standard of living

Why use the HDI, not just income?

Kenya

Income per person: US$5,608
Life expectancy: 63.6 years
HDI: 0.628

Nigeria

Income per person: US$5,569
Life expectancy: 54.5 years
HDI: 0.560

Almost the same income, but people in Kenya can expect to live 9.1 years longer, and Kenya’s HDI is higher. Income alone would hide this difference.

Source: United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), Human Development Report 2025, Table 1 (data for 2023).

Cold callSo why do geographers often use the HDI rather than income per person? Because the HDI includes health and education as well as income, so it gives a fuller picture of wellbeing.

Step 4: Explain why an indicator is useful (2 marks)

Explain why life expectancy is useful for comparing human wellbeing between countries. (2 marks)

What it measures → why that matters for wellbeing → an example from the table.
Life expectancy measures how long people can expect to live, which reflects their access to healthcare, good nutrition and safe living conditions. For example, people in Norway can expect to live 22.1 years longer than people in Niger (83.3 years compared with 61.2), which suggests that health and wellbeing are much better in Norway.

2 marks: what the indicator shows, linked to wellbeing (an example strengthens it). 1 mark: describes the indicator only.

Step 5: Explain a limitation of national averages (2 marks)

Explain one problem with using a country’s average income to judge the wellbeing of all its people. (2 marks)

An average is one number for a whole country. It hides differences between rich and poor, cities and rural areas, regions, men and women.
A national average gives one figure for the whole country, so it hides differences within it. For example, Brazil’s average income per person of US$18,011 combines the incomes of very rich and very poor families, and of wealthy cities and poorer rural areas. As a result, the average may make wellbeing look better than it is for Brazil’s poorest people.

Gesture voteThumbs up or down: “Norway’s high HDI means every person in Norway has high wellbeing.” Thumbs down: it is an average and hides differences between people.

Check and improve your answers

(a) Somalia (58.8 years) (1 mark)

(b) 82.1 − 58.8 = 23.3 years (1 mark; the unit is needed)

(c) Sample 2-mark answer

Income per person measures how much money people have on average, which shows what they can afford, such as housing, food and healthcare. For example, the average income in New Zealand (US$47,260) is about 32 times that of Somalia (US$1,475), which suggests a much higher standard of living in New Zealand.

(d) Sample 2-mark answer

An average gives one number for a whole country, so it hides differences between groups of people. For example, life expectancy in Fiji is an average, so people in remote villages with fewer hospitals may live shorter lives than people in the cities, even though the average looks the same for everyone.

Extension: Nepal’s HDI (0.622) is lower than Fiji’s (0.731), but its life expectancy is higher (70.4 compared with 67.3 years). The HDI combines three dimensions, so Fiji’s much higher income per person (US$12,843 compared with US$4,726) outweighs Nepal’s longer life expectancy.
